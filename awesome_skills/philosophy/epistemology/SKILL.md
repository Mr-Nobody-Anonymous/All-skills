# Epistemology

## Scope
Epistemology is the branch of philosophy concerned with the nature, sources, and limits of knowledge. It addresses what we can know, how we can know it, and what makes beliefs justified or rational.

## The Classical Analysis of Knowledge

### Justified True Belief (JTB)
S knows that p if and only if:
1. p is **true**
2. S **believes** that p
3. S is **justified** in believing that p

### Gettier Problem (1963)
Gettier cases show JTB is insufficient — one can have justified true belief that isn't knowledge:
- Example: Smith is justified in believing Jones will get the job (boss said so) and that Jones has 10 coins in his pocket (Smith counted them). Smith infers "the person who will get the job has 10 coins." But Smith, not Jones, gets the job, and Smith happens to have 10 coins. His belief is justified and true, but not knowledge.

### Post-Gettier Responses
- **No false lemmas**: Knowledge = JTB where justification doesn't depend on any false belief
- **Defeasibility**: Knowledge = JTB where no true evidence would defeat the justification
- **Causal theory**: Knowledge requires appropriate causal connection between belief and fact
- **Reliabilism**: Knowledge = true belief produced by a reliable process
- **Virtue epistemology**: Knowledge = true belief arising from intellectual virtues (competence, care)

## Sources of Knowledge

### Perception
- **Direct realism**: We perceive external objects directly
- **Representationalism**: We perceive mental representations caused by external objects
- **Problem**: Argument from illusion, hallucination, perceptual variation

### Reason
- **A priori knowledge**: Known independently of experience (logic, mathematics)
- **A posteriori knowledge**: Known through experience (empirical)
- **Rationalism**: Reason is a primary source of knowledge (Descartes, Leibniz, Spinoza)
- **Empiricism**: Experience is the primary source (Locke, Hume, Berkeley)

### Testimony
- **Reductionism**: Testimony justified only if we have independent reasons to trust the testifier
- **Anti-reductionism**: Testimony is a basic source of knowledge (like perception)
- **Social epistemology**: Knowledge as distributed across communities

### Memory, Introspection, Inference
- Memory preserves but doesn't generate new justification
- Introspection provides privileged access to mental states (debated)
- Inference: Deductive (certainty-preserving), inductive (probability-raising), abductive (best explanation)

## Skepticism

### Types
- **Global**: No knowledge is possible (Pyrrhonian skepticism)
- **External world**: Can't know anything about the external world (brain-in-a-vat)
- **Inductive**: Can't justify inductive inferences (Hume's problem)

### Major Arguments
- **Regress problem**: Every justification requires further justification → infinite regress, circularity, or foundationalism
- **Dream argument** (Descartes): Can't distinguish dreaming from waking
- **Evil demon** (Descartes): Systematic deception is logically possible

### Responses
- **Foundationalism**: Some beliefs are self-justifying (basic beliefs)
- **Coherentism**: Beliefs justified by coherence with the belief system as a whole
- **Pragmatism**: Skepticism is practically self-defeating
- **Contextualism**: Knowledge attributions vary with the standards of the conversational context

## Contemporary Debates
- **Epistemic injustice** (Fricker): Testimonial injustice, hermeneutical injustice
- **Epistemic responsibility**: Duties to form beliefs carefully, avoid bias
- **Formal epistemology**: Bayesian updating, probabilistic reasoning
- **Naturalized epistemology** (Quine): Epistemology as continuous with empirical science

## Key Figures
- Plato, Descartes, Locke, Hume, Kant, Russell, Quine, Gettier, Goldman, Sosa, Zagzebski, Fricker
